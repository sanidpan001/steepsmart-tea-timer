The complete guide to tea steeping times and temperatures

Two variables decide whether your cup tastes like a revelation or a regret: water temperature and steep time. Get them right and even an everyday green tea turns silky, floral and sweet. Get them wrong and the same leaves taste harsh, flat or stubbornly bitter. This guide explains exactly why these two numbers matter so much, gives you a practical tea steeping time chart for every major category, and shows how a gongfu tea timer and a green tea temperature timer can take the guesswork out of every brew.

All steeping data on this page is baked into SteepSmart as guidance compiled from public brewing instructions published by tea vendors and educators. Treat the numbers as reliable starting points — leaf grade, harvest season, water mineral content and your own palate all nudge the ideal values a little. The built-in timer, infusion tracker and temperature helper above turn those starting points into repeatable, delicious cups.

Why temperature and time matter more than the tea itself

Tea leaves contain hundreds of compounds, but three families dominate flavor: caffeine (bitter and stimulating), catechins and polyphenols (bitter and astringent), and amino acids such as L-theanine (sweet, savory and relaxing). These compounds dissolve at different rates and at different temperatures. Hot water yanks caffeine and catechins out of the leaf quickly, which is why a delicate green tea steeped in boiling water for five minutes tastes punishingly bitter. Cooler water extracts amino acids more favorably relative to bitter compounds, which is why the same green tea steeped at 75°C for two minutes tastes sweet and brothy instead.

Time works the same way. A short infusion captures the most soluble, most aromatic compounds first — the florals, the sweetness, the brightness. As the steep stretches on, heavier bitter compounds keep dissolving while the delicate ones stop contributing much. That is the entire logic of gongfu brewing: many short steeps, each one capturing a different slice of the leaf's character, instead of one long steep that blurs everything into bitterness.

Western brewing vs gongfu brewing

Western-style brewing is what most of the world does every day: a few grams of leaf in a large teapot or mug, one long steep of two to five minutes, then the whole cup is drunk at once. It is simple, forgiving and perfect for breakfast black teas and herbal tisanes. A green tea temperature timer matters enormously here, because you cannot fix an over-extracted western brew — there is no second chance.

Gongfu brewing is the Chinese method of making tea: a high ratio of leaf to water in a small gaiwan or clay pot, with many short infusions that lengthen as the session goes on. A floral Tieguanyin oolong might run 15 seconds, then 25, then 35, building across six or seven rounds. A gongfu tea timer is almost essential for this style, because the differences between a 10-second and a 25-second steep are dramatic, and keeping track of which infusion you are on — while also jotting flavor notes — is genuinely hard without help. Green tea temperature timer: the numbers that matter most

If there is one category where a green tea temperature timer earns its place, it is green tea. The difference between 75°C and 100°C is the difference between sweet and scorched. Sencha peaks around 75°C for two minutes. Dragon Well and gunpowder prefer 80°C. Hojicha, being roasted, tolerates 90°C for a short one-minute steep. And gyokuro — the shade-grown luxury green — demands water as cool as 55°C, barely warm to the touch, for a two-to-three-minute steep that yields a thick, umami-rich liquor.

Most people do not own a variable-temperature kettle, which is exactly why SteepSmart includes the boil-then-wait helper. Boil your kettle, enter your target — say 80°C for Dragon Well — pick your vessel, and the tool estimates how many minutes to let the water cool. As a rule of thumb, water in a closed kettle drops from 100°C to about 90°C in four to five minutes, and to 80°C in eight to nine; an open glass cup gets there roughly twice as fast. The helper models this properly with Newton's law of cooling so you get a number tuned to your actual vessel.

How to run a gongfu session with the timer

A first gongfu session can feel intimidating, but the rhythm is simple once a timer handles the counting. Warm your gaiwan or small teapot with hot water and discard it. Add leaf — roughly 5 to 7 grams per 100 ml for oolongs and pu-erh, a little less for greens. If the tea calls for it (pu-erh and many oolongs do), do a quick 5-second rinse and discard it; this wakes the leaves up rather than being the first drinkable steep.

Then start the first infusion on the timer. Pour water, start the countdown, and decant every drop into a pitcher or cup when the seconds run out — leaving water on the leaves is the most common beginner mistake, because the steep keeps going. Taste, jot a note in the infusion log, and hit the log button: the timer automatically advances to the next infusion with its slightly longer countdown. Repeat until the flavor thins out, which is your honest signal that the session is over.

One more tip: keep your notes short but specific. "Honey, orchid, sweet finish" is more useful than "good." Over a few sessions you will start to see your own palate's patterns — which infusions you love, which teas deserve reordering — and that is where the saved log becomes genuinely valuable.

Caffeine: what to expect per cup

Tea's caffeine range is wide. Herbal tisanes — chamomile, peppermint, rooibos, hibiscus — contain essentially none. Green teas sit around 25 to 40 mg per 8 oz cup, though gyokuro can reach 120 mg because of its shade-grown intensity. Oolongs land at 45 to 55 mg, black teas at 50 to 70 mg, and pu-erh around 65 to 70 mg. For comparison, a typical cup of coffee carries about 95 mg, so most teas are gentler but not negligible.

In gongfu brewing the caffeine picture changes across the session: the first infusion extracts the most caffeine, and each later round carries progressively less. SteepSmart's estimator models this decay and totals your whole session, so a seven-infusion oolong session lands far below seven times the first cup's caffeine. If you are caffeine-sensitive, favor white teas, hojicha and herbals later in the day, and check the per-cup estimate before you brew.

Common mistakes (and easy fixes)

  • Boiling water on green tea. The single most common error. Use the temperature helper or let the kettle rest — greens want 75–85°C.
  • Leaving the leaves in. In western brewing, remove or decant at the timer's end. In gongfu, decant completely every round.
  • Too little leaf in gongfu. Gongfu needs a generous leaf ratio; skimping gives thin, boring infusions no timer can fix.
  • Judging all infusions by the first. The second and third steeps are often the sweetest. Give the session its full arc before deciding.
  • Skipping the rinse. For pu-erh and roasted oolongs, a quick rinse dramatically improves the first drinkable infusion.

Water, storage and the details that add up

Great timing cannot rescue bad water. Heavily chlorinated tap water mutes delicate teas; a simple carbon filter is the cheapest upgrade in tea. Store tea airtight, cool and dark — green teas fade fastest and are best within months of harvest, while pu-erh and some oolongs age gracefully. And preheating your vessel matters more than people think: pouring 80°C water into a cold ceramic pot can drop the actual steeping temperature by several degrees, so a quick hot-water rinse of the empty pot keeps your carefully timed temperature honest.

Ultimately, brewing great tea is a practice, not a formula. Use SteepSmart's tea steeping time chart as your starting line, let the gongfu tea timer keep your rhythm, check the green tea temperature timer before you pour, and keep notes. Within a few sessions you will stop needing the chart for your favorites — but you will keep reaching for the timer, because some pleasures are worth measuring precisely.

Frequently asked questions

How long should I steep green tea?

Most green teas steep best for 2 to 3 minutes in western-style brewing at 75 to 80°C. Sencha does well at 75°C for 2 minutes, Dragon Well at 80°C for 3 minutes, and delicate gyokuro at only 55°C for about 2 to 3 minutes. Longer or hotter steeps pull out bitter tannins. SteepSmart's built-in tea steeping time chart gives the exact recommendation for 42 teas.

What is the right water temperature for green tea?

Green tea generally wants 75 to 85°C, never a rolling boil. Sencha and genmaicha prefer about 75°C, Dragon Well and gunpowder about 80°C, hojicha up to 90°C, and shade-grown gyokuro as low as 55°C. How does a gongfu tea timer work?

Gongfu brewing uses a small vessel and many short infusions instead of one long steep. You pick a tea, and the gongfu tea timer counts down each infusion — for example, a Tieguanyin oolong might go 15, 25, 35 seconds and so on — adding a few seconds each round. SteepSmart automatically advances the timer through up to 10 infusions and lets you log each one with flavor notes.

How many times can you reinfuse oolong tea?

High-quality oolongs are the champions of reinfusing. Da Hong Pao can give 8 good infusions, Tieguanyin and Phoenix Dancong about 7, and milk oolong around 6. Green teas usually fade after 3, white teas after 4 to 5, and pu-erh can go to 8 or 10. Stop when the flavor thins out — SteepSmart shows the expected infusion count for each tea so you know what to aim for.

How much caffeine is in a cup of tea?

A standard 8 oz cup ranges from zero in herbal tisanes to about 120 mg in gyokuro. Typical green teas carry 25 to 40 mg, oolongs 45 to 55 mg, and black teas 50 to 70 mg — roughly half a cup of coffee. Real results vary with leaf grade, harvest, water mineral content and personal taste, so treat the times and temperatures as a reliable starting point and adjust to your palate — or log your own variations in the infusion notes.
